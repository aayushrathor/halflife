radioactive decay, but it's your lifespan

the theory

Radioactive atoms don't age. Each one has a fixed chance of decaying per year, no matter how old it is. What if humans worked the same way? No aging — just a fixed annual chance of "decaying." Pick a long enough half-life and you're functionally immortal, just not guaranteed.

N(t) = N₀ · e^(-λt)    λ = ln(2) / half-life

fun fact bank

  • Odds of lightning strike (US, per year): about 1 in 1,222,000
  • Odds of a vending machine death: about 1 in 112,000,000
  • Half-life of caffeine in your body: 5-6 hours
  • Half-life of Carbon-14: 5,730 years — archaeology's favorite clock
  • Half-life of a muon: 2.2 microseconds, the fastest decay around

more about radioactivity

  • Marie Curie coined the term "radioactivity" in 1898 while studying uranium ores, and discovered polonium and radium along the way.
  • Bananas are mildly radioactive because of potassium-40 — the running joke unit "banana equivalent dose" compares everyday radiation exposure to eating one banana.
  • Technetium-99m, with a half-life of about 6 hours, is one of the most widely used isotopes in medical imaging because it decays almost fully out of the body within a couple of days.
  • Some isotopes have half-lives measured in fractions of a second (like polonium-214 at 164 microseconds), while others, like bismuth-209, take over a billion times the age of the universe to decay.
  • Smoke detectors commonly contain a tiny amount of americium-241, which has a half-life of about 432 years.
  • The half-lives of different isotopes span more than 40 orders of magnitude — from microseconds to longer than the universe has existed.
  • Radioactive decay is truly random at the level of a single atom — nobody can predict when one specific atom will decay, only the odds for a large group over time.
